Gradient descent, intuitively

Promise

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain, in plain words and with one line of code, why a neural network's weights move downhill — and predict what happens when the steps are too big.

Imagine standing on a hillside in thick fog. You can't see the valley, but you can feel the slope under your feet. Gradient descent is the strategy of always stepping slightly downhill.

The loss function is that hillside. Each weight in the network is a coordinate of your position, and the loss is your altitude — how wrong the model currently is. You can't jump to the bottom, because you don't know where it is. What you can do is measure the local slope: the gradient. It points uphill, so you step the other way.

One step looks like this. The learning rate lr is your step size; the gradient grad is the slope where you stand:

# one step of gradient descent
w = w - lr * grad
# then measure how wrong we are now
loss = mse(predict(x, w), y)

Step size is the whole game. Too small, and you shuffle for hours without reaching the valley. Too big, and you leap over the valley floor entirely — the loss goes up, and the next step leaps even further. That divergence is the most common failure you'll meet in practice, and it's why you'll compute a step by hand in the next activity before trusting any framework to do it for you.

Check your understanding

You run one training step and the loss increases sharply. What is the most likely cause?

The model has memorized the training data.
The learning rate is too large, so the step overshot the valley.
The gradient was computed on too few examples.
Right. A rising loss after a step is the classic signature of overshooting — the step was longer than the valley is wide. Halving the learning rate is the usual first move.
Not quite. Think about what happens to your position on the hillside if your step is much longer than the valley is wide.
