Step 1 · Text + images

The hillside you can't see

Imagine standing on a hillside in thick fog. You can't see the valley, but you can feel the slope under your feet. Gradient descent is the strategy of always stepping slightly downhill.

The loss function is that hillside. Each weight in the network is a coordinate of your position, and the loss is your altitude — how wrong the model currently is. You can't jump to the bottom, because you don't know where it is. What you can do is measure the local slope: the gradient. It points uphill, so you step the other way.

Step size is the whole game. Too small, and you shuffle for hours without reaching the valley. Too big, and you leap over the valley floor entirely — the loss goes up, and the next step leaps even further. The next step lets you feel that difference with your own hands.

Step 2 · Simulation

Feel the step size

Drag the learning rate and watch forty training steps on a simple one-weight model. The curve shows the loss after each step.

lr = 0.30

A correct tiny simulation: one weight, loss = w², gradient = 2w, update w = w − lr · 2w. Above lr = 1 the steps overshoot and the loss diverges.

Step 3 · Role play · Mock interview

Explain it back

A mock interview: answer in your own words before you peek at the sample. The interviewer prompt and the sample response were both written ahead of time.

Interviewer · prewritten prompt

"Suppose you train a model and, right after one step, the loss jumps up instead of down. In your own words — what's the first thing you'd suspect, and why?"

Sample response · prewritten sample — no live AI

"The first thing I'd suspect is the learning rate. Each step moves the weights by lr times the gradient, and if that step is longer than the valley is wide, the model lands on the far wall, higher than where it started — so the loss goes up. Halving the learning rate is the usual first move, and I'd watch the next few steps to confirm the loss falls again."
