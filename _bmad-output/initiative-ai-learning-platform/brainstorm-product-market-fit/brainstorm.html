02 / Five Whys

Below the symptom.

Six hypothetical ladders, each following its previous answer through five levels. These are causal explanations to investigate, not interview findings or established root causes.

A / Hypothetical Learning transferFive levels · interventions WHY01–05

Symptom to investigate: Learners finish lessons but struggle to make engineering decisions.

  1. Why might lesson completion fail to transfer?

    Because the activity may reward recognizing an explanation rather than making a decision.

  2. Why might it reward recognition?

    Because content checks may ask what a term means instead of making learners choose under constraints.

  3. Why might constraints be absent?

    Because realistic situations can be expensive to design and assess.

  4. Why might that expense remain high?

    Because each situation may be authored and evaluated as a one-off.

  5. Why might each be one-off?

    Because the teaching workflow may lack reusable structures linking objectives, choices, consequences and rubrics; investigate this workflow bottleneck.

WHY01–05: decision patterns → boundary conditions

B / Hypothetical AI dependenceFive levels · interventions WHY06–10

Symptom to investigate: AI-assisted learners can produce correct artifacts they cannot explain.

  1. Why might a correct artifact hide weak understanding?

    Because an AI assistant can supply the artifact without the learner making its key decisions.

  2. Why might the learner accept that substitution?

    Because finishing the task may feel more urgent than understanding the decision.

  3. Why might finishing dominate?

    Because visible completion may receive clearer rewards than reasoning.

  4. Why might reasoning receive weak rewards?

    Because assessment may inspect the final artifact without examining how it was chosen.

  5. Why might assessment focus only on the artifact?

    Because process evidence can be hard to capture and judge fairly; investigate evidence design rather than assuming laziness.

WHY06–10: checkpoints → risk-prioritized checks

C / Hypothetical Inclusive entry pointsFive levels · interventions WHY11–15

Symptom to investigate: Non-CS learners disengage from software engineering instruction.

  1. Why might they disengage?

    Because examples may require unfamiliar vocabulary before the practical value is visible.

  2. Why might vocabulary come first?

    Because the course may inherit a sequence designed for CS students.

  3. Why might that sequence persist?

    Because rewriting separate courses for different backgrounds increases teaching work.

  4. Why might separate rewrites seem necessary?

    Because background differences may be treated as entirely different objectives rather than different entry points.

  5. Why might objectives and entry points be conflated?

    Because personalization may lack a shared competency model; investigate whether shared outcomes with different representations work.

WHY11–15: familiar bridges → prerequisite repair

D / Hypothetical Instructor trustFive levels · interventions WHY16–20

Symptom to investigate: Instructors hesitate to use generated learning material.

  1. Why might instructors hesitate?

    Because they remain accountable for accuracy and educational quality.

  2. Why might that accountability block adoption?

    Because reviewing a generated activity may take as much effort as creating one.

  3. Why might reviewing take so long?

    Because the output may mix claims, examples and assessments without showing their origin.

  4. Why might origin be unclear?

    Because generation may optimize a coherent narrative without exposing evidence and uncertainty.

  5. Why might hidden uncertainty persist?

    Because the authoring interface may treat fluent text as finished teaching content; investigate a review-oriented workflow.

WHY16–20: risk review → approved exemplars

E / Hypothetical Voluntary returnFive levels · interventions WHY21–25

Symptom to investigate: A platform gets trial use but little voluntary return.

  1. Why might people not return?

    Because the first experience may solve a one-off curiosity rather than a recurring task.

  2. Why might a one-off task be targeted?

    Because the product may be positioned around a broad topic rather than a repeated moment of need.

  3. Why might positioning stay broad?

    Because many audience types can say the concept sounds useful.

  4. Why might broad approval be misleading?

    Because surveys about imagined value are easier to obtain than observations of actual choice.

  5. Why might actual choice be missing?

    Because early validation may avoid asking people to switch behavior, allocate time or pay; investigate revealed demand.

WHY21–25: real episodes → rejection reasons

F / Hypothetical Engagement ≠ learningFive levels · interventions WHY26–30

Symptom to investigate: More engaging activities do not necessarily produce better learning.

  1. Why might engagement fail to improve learning?

    Because visible participation can occur without effortful reasoning.

  2. Why might reasoning be avoided?

    Because rapid hints, points or feedback can let learners progress without committing to a prediction.

  3. Why might progression permit that?

    Because the system may optimize completion and session activity.

  4. Why might activity be the optimization target?

    Because it is easier to count than capability or later transfer.

  5. Why might capability remain unmeasured?

    Because the product may lack an explicit learning claim and matching assessment; investigate what it promises to change.

WHY26–30: learning claims → outcome comparison

Demand & target audience QS01–10 / 10 questions
  1. QS01

    Who encountered this learning problem in the last month rather than merely liking the idea of a new platform?

  2. QS02

    What specific task were they trying to accomplish when their current approach became inadequate?

  3. QS03

    How frequently does that situation recur for the same person or teaching team?

  4. QS04

    What consequence makes the problem worth addressing now?

  5. QS05

    Which differences between non-CS learners and CS graduates are truly needs rather than demographic labels?

  6. QS06

    Who experiences the pain, who selects the tool, and who can authorize spending?

  7. QS07

    How would we find people who rejected the concept rather than interviewing only enthusiastic classmates?

  8. QS08

    What behavior would distinguish curiosity about AI from a recurring educational need?

  9. QS09

    Which audience can we actually reach for repeated observation during the course project?

  10. QS10

    What would make a learner choose to practice when there is no grade or reward attached?

Alternatives & differentiation QS11–20 / 10 questions

  2. QS12

    Which parts of those approaches already work well enough that replacing them would create little value?

    What prevents a learner from reproducing the proposed experience with a well-written prompt?

  4. QS14

    What switching cost would outweigh an improvement in learning experience?

  5. QS15

    What evidence would show that realistic simulations help more than a simpler worked example?

  6. QS16

    Which imagined gap is actually absent from current tools, and which have we simply not investigated?

  7. QS17

  8. QS18

  9. QS19

    What would remain valuable if content generation became essentially free?

  10. QS20

  4. QS24

    Who determines whether an AI feedback judgment is wrong, unfair or too uncertain?

  5. QS25

  6. QS26

  7. QS27

  8. QS28

  9. QS29

  10. QS30

08 / The field-test notebook · not executed

Test the value.
Not the applause.

Before pitching, reconstruct recent struggles and observe current alternatives. Then test demand, added value, learning, repeated choice and viability separately.

Illustrative pilot, not a benchmark: the log suggests a feasible exploratory sample such as 8–12 learners across backgrounds and 3–5 instructors. A convenience sample can expose usability failures and disconfirm assumptions; it cannot establish population demand or PMF. No numbers in this brainstorm are industry thresholds.

Required use
Course participation can be compulsory. It demonstrates attendance or compliance, not voluntary demand.
Engagement
Activity, completion and satisfaction are proxies. Busy clicking, game points and increased confidence do not prove capability.
Learning
Look for a specific capability and transfer to an unfamiliar case. Separate assisted performance from unaided performance; assessment design needs expert review.
Retention
Look for repeated voluntary choice after mandatory participation ends, and independent instructor reuse. Reminders, prizes and grade credit can distort the signal.
Product–market fit
Sustained audience value supported by outcomes, voluntary return, referrals and adoption/payment evidence. A tiny pilot or successful course demo is not enough; stronger conclusions need multiple cohorts and longitudinal evidence.
E1 / DEMAND

Find a real recurring episode.

Reconstruct recent learning or teaching episodes: alternatives, frequency, stakes and effort. Seek a concrete commitment to try a relevant mission. Do not substitute “would you use it?” surveys for observation.

Disconfirming signalThe task rarely occurs, current alternatives are sufficient, or people will not allocate time.

E2 / ADDED VALUE

Give the simpler alternative a fair chance.

Manually facilitate one mission or a low-fidelity prototype. Compare with an equivalent worked example or well-prompted AI chat, keeping content, time and target capability as comparable as possible. Collect reasoning, perceived effort and failure cases.

Signal against the value hypothesisThe mission offers no advantage over the simpler exercise or chat alternative—the scenario-quality risk named in the synthesis. A tiny exploratory comparison cannot support a strong causal claim.

E3 / LEARNING

Change the context, then check again.

Use a pre-task, a changed-context post-task and, if feasible, a later task with an instructor-reviewed rubric. Separate assisted from unassisted performance, inspect disagreements and avoid high-stakes grading by AI.

Disconfirming signalOnly confidence or completion increases, or performance does not transfer.

E4 / REPEATED CHOICE

Watch what happens after “required.”

Offer a relevant second mission without grade credit or prizes after mandatory activity ends. Separately observe whether an instructor prepares and assigns a second activity. Record invitations, voluntary starts/completions and reasons for no return.

Signal against recurring valueNo independent second use, or returns that depend on compulsory coursework, reminders or incentives. These do not establish retention.

E5 / VIABILITY

Count the whole cost, and find the actual buyer.

Measure total instructor setup and review effort and cost per completed mission, including failed generations, retries, narration and support. Identify the budget holder and ask for a realistic next adoption step. Willingness to pay remains untested without a credible commitment, transaction or institutional allocation.

Signals against viabilityReview effort exceeds authoring savings, costs fail the ceiling to be agreed before the pilot, or no credible adoption/payment commitment emerges. A buyer and procurement route remain unresolved.

Write the decision rules before seeing the results. Agree with the user what quality improvement matters, how much preparation time is tolerable, what independent reuse counts and what cost ceiling is acceptable. None of these thresholds has been approved in this brainstorm.
